# Reed–Solomon Proximity

In the previous chapter, we introduced Reed–Solomon codes through a very simple idea:

$$
\boxed{
\text{low-degree polynomial}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{evaluation vector}
}
$$

Given an evaluation domain

$$
D=\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\},
$$

the Reed–Solomon code is

$$
RS(D,k)
=
\left\{
(f(x_1),\ldots,f(x_n))
:f\in F[x] \quad\&\quad
\deg f<k
\right\}.
$$

We also saw why these codes have large minimum distance.

If

$$
f\neq g
$$

are two polynomials of degree less than $k$, then

$$
f-g
$$

is a nonzero polynomial of degree less than $k$, so it can have at most $k-1$ roots.

Therefore their evaluation vectors can agree at at most $k-1$ positions.

This gives

$$
d_{\min}=n-k+1.
$$

So far, our story has been the classical coding-theory story:

> A codeword was transmitted, some coordinates were corrupted, and we want to recover the original codeword.

But FRI is going to ask a slightly different question.

Instead of being given a corrupted codeword, suppose someone simply gives us a function

$$
g:D\to\mathbb F.
$$

We want to know:

> **Does this function behave like a low-degree polynomial?**

And perhaps an even more interesting question:

> **If it is not exactly a low-degree polynomial, how close is it to one?**

This is the idea of **Reed–Solomon proximity**.

---

# Distance from a Function to a Code

Let

$$
g:D\to\mathbb F
$$

be an arbitrary function. We can think of $g$ as an evaluation vector:

$$
g=
(g(x_1),\ldots,g(x_n)).
$$

The Reed–Solomon code consists of all vectors generated by low-degree polynomials.

So define the distance from $g$ to the code as

$$
\boxed{
d_H(g,RS(D,k))
=
\min_{f\in RS(D,k)}
d_H(g,f).
}
$$

In words:

> The distance from $g$ to the Reed–Solomon code is the minimum number of coordinates that must be changed to turn $g$ into a valid Reed–Solomon codeword.

Equivalently, we can write

$$
d_H(g,RS(D,k))
=
\min_{\deg f<k}
\left|
\{x\in D:g(x)\neq f(x)\}
\right|.
$$

This formulation makes the polynomial connection explicit.

We are asking:

> Among all polynomials of degree less than $k$, which one agrees with $g$ on the largest number of points?

---

# A Simple Example

Suppose

$$
D=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
$$

and our degree bound is

$$
\deg f<3.
$$

Suppose we are given the function

$$
g=
(2,5,10,17,26,37).
$$

Look carefully at these values. They come from

$$
f(X)=X^2+1.
$$

Indeed,

$$
f(1)=2,
$$

$$
f(2)=5,
$$

$$
f(3)=10,
$$

and so on.

Therefore

$$
g\in RS(D,3).
$$

Its distance from the code is

$$
d_H(g,RS(D,3))=0.
$$

There is no correction necessary. The function is already a low-degree polynomial evaluated on $D$.

---

# Now Introduce an Error

Suppose instead we receive

$$
g=
(2,5,10,18,26,37).
$$

Everything is the same except the value at $x=4$. The original polynomial was

$$
f(X)=X^2+1,
$$

for which

$$
f(4)=17.
$$

But our function has

$$
g(4)=18.
$$

Therefore $g$ is not exactly a Reed–Solomon codeword. However, it differs from the codeword generated by $f$ at only one position.

So

$$
d_H(g,RS(D,3))=1.
$$

So $g$ is **close** to the Reed–Solomon code even though it is not itself a codeword.

This is the central idea of proximity.

---

# Normalized Distance

There is one problem with using ordinary Hamming distance.

Suppose one function differs from a codeword in \(2\) positions out of \(10\).

Another differs in \(20\) positions out of \(100\).

Both have distance \(2\) or \(20\), but intuitively they have the same fraction of errors:

$$
\frac{2}{10}
=
\frac{20}{100}
=
0.2.
$$

So for large codes, it is more useful to normalize the distance.

Define the **relative Hamming distance** between two functions $g,h:D\to\mathbb F_q$ by

$$
\boxed{
\Delta(g,h)
=
\frac{d_H(g,h)}{|D|}.
}
$$

Then define the relative distance from $g$ to the Reed–Solomon code:

$$
\boxed{
\Delta(g,RS(D,k))
=
\min_{c\in RS(D,k)}
\Delta(g,c).
}
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{
\Delta(g,RS(D,k))
=
\min_{\deg f<k}
\frac{
|\{x\in D:g(x)\neq f(x)\}|
}{|D|}.
}
$$

This tells us the **fraction of domain points on which $g$ must change** before it becomes a low-degree polynomial.

---

# What Does “Close” Mean?

Suppose

$$
\Delta(g,RS(D,k))=0.01.
$$

This means there exists a polynomial

$$
f,\qquad\deg f<k,
$$

such that

$$
g(x)=f(x)
$$

on \(99\%\) of the points in $D$.

So we can say:

> $g$ is \(1\%\) away from the Reed–Solomon code.

Similarly, if

$$
\Delta(g,RS(D,k))=0.4,
$$

then at least \(40\%\) of the values must be changed to obtain a degree-$<k$ polynomial evaluation.

The distinction is therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\Delta=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{exactly low-degree}
}
$$

while

$$
\boxed{
\Delta\text{ small}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{close to low-degree}.
}
$$

This distinction is fundamental for FRI.

---

# The Closest Polynomial

Suppose

$$
g:D\to\mathbb F.
$$

We can define the set of closest Reed–Solomon codewords:

$$
\operatorname{argmin}_{c\in RS(D,k)}
d_H(g,c).
$$

Equivalently, we are looking for a polynomial

$$
f
$$

of degree less than $k$ that agrees with $g$ on as many points as possible.

This gives us another useful formulation:

$$
\boxed{
\text{RS proximity}
=
\text{maximum agreement with a low-degree polynomial}.
}
$$

Instead of counting disagreements, we can count agreements.

If $g$ and $f$ agree at $A$ points of $D$, then

$$
d_H(g,f)=n-A,\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{where}\qquad |D|=n.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\Delta(g,f)
=
1-\frac{A}{n}.
$$

So asking whether $g$ is close to a Reed–Solomon codeword is equivalent to asking:

> **Is there a low-degree polynomial that agrees with $g$ on almost all of the domain?**

This language of **agreement** will be useful when we later discuss low-degree testing.

---

# Why Can't Two Low-Degree Polynomials Both Be Very Close?

Suppose $g$ is close to two different low-degree polynomials:

$$
f \qquad\text{and}\quad h
$$

Can that happen?

The answer depends on the minimum distance of the Reed–Solomon code.

Recall:

$$
d_{\min}=n-k+1.
$$

Therefore any two distinct Reed–Solomon codewords differ in at least

$$
n-k+1
$$

positions. In another word:

$$
d_H(f,h)\ge n-k+1.
$$

In relative terms,

$$
\boxed{
\Delta(f,h)
\ge
\frac{n-k+1}{n}.
}
$$

Now suppose $g$ is close to both.

By the triangle inequality,

$$
\Delta(f,h)
\le
\Delta(f,g)+\Delta(g,h).
$$

Therefore, if

$$
\Delta(f,g)+\Delta(g,h)
<
\frac{n-k+1}{n},
$$

then $f$ and $h$ would have to be closer than the minimum distance allows.

That is impossible.

So a function cannot be very close to two different Reed–Solomon codewords at the same time.

This is the geometric meaning of minimum distance.

---

# The Unique Decoding Radius

Recall from Chapter 1 that a code with minimum distance $d$ can uniquely correct up to

$$
t=\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor
$$

errors.

For Reed–Solomon,

$$
d=n-k+1.
$$

Therefore the unique decoding radius is

$$
\boxed{
\left\lfloor
\frac{n-k}{2}
\right\rfloor.
}
$$

In relative terms, this is approximately

$$
\frac{n-k}{2n}.
$$

This tells us something important about proximity.

If a function is sufficiently close to the Reed–Solomon code, then there is a **unique** nearby codeword.

But FRI will eventually deal with a slightly different problem.

It does not necessarily want to recover that closest polynomial.

It wants to **verify that a claimed function is close to a low-degree polynomial without having to reconstruct the polynomial directly**.

That is a much more interesting problem.

---

# Membership Is Easy; Proximity Is Harder

Suppose someone gives us

$$
g:D\to\mathbb F.
$$

There are two possible tasks.

### Task 1: Exact membership

Determine whether

$$
g\in RS(D,k).
$$

If we have enough evaluations, we can interpolate a polynomial and check whether the remaining values agree with it.

### Task 2: Proximity

Determine whether

$$
\Delta(g,RS(D,k))
$$

is small.

This is harder. Why?

Because now we are not simply checking whether one polynomial explains all the values.

We are asking whether **some** low-degree polynomial explains almost all of them.

In principle, we could try every possible polynomial. But that is obviously infeasible for a large field. So we need a more clever way. And this is where **low-degree testing** enters.

---

# From Proximity to Low-Degree Testing

Suppose we have a function

$$
g:D\to\mathbb F.
$$

We want to distinguish between two situations.

### Case A: $g$ is close to low-degree

There exists some polynomial $f$ with

$$
\deg f<k
$$

such that

$$
g
$$

agrees with $f$ on most of $D$.

### Case B: $g$ is far from low-degree

Every polynomial of degree less than $k$ disagrees with $g$ on a significant fraction of $D$.

So we want a procedure that can determine which situation we are in. Ideally, we do not want to read all $n$ values of $g$. Instead, we want to inspect a small number of values and obtain convincing evidence about the global property:

$$
\boxed{
\text{“Is }g\text{ close to a low-degree polynomial?”}
}
$$

This is the basic idea behind a **low-degree test**.

And FRI is fundamentally a protocol for this kind of task.

---

# Why Can't We Just Interpolate?

At first, a natural question is:

> Why don't we simply interpolate the polynomial and check everything?

Suppose we know $k$ values of $g$ at distinct points.

There is always a unique polynomial $f$ of degree less than $k$ passing through those points. So we could interpolate $f$.

But this does **not** prove that $g$ is low-degree. Why?

Because any $k$ arbitrary points can be explained by a degree-$<k$ polynomial.

The real question is whether the **entire function** agrees with that polynomial.

For example, suppose

$$
k=3.
$$

Take three arbitrary values:

$$
g(x_1),g(x_2),g(x_3).
$$

There is always a quadratic polynomial

$$
f
$$

such that

$$
f(x_i)=g(x_i)
$$

for all three points.

But if $g$ is arbitrary, it may disagree with $f$ everywhere else.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{interpolating a few points is not the same as proving low degree}.
}
$$

This distinction is essential.

---

# A Function Can Look Low-Degree Locally

This leads to a subtle but important idea.

Every sufficiently small collection of points can be explained by a low-degree polynomial.

For example:

* 1 point determines a constant polynomial;
* 2 points determine a line;
* 3 points determine a quadratic;
* $k$ points determine a polynomial of degree less than $k$.

Therefore, simply finding a polynomial that agrees with a few samples tells us very little.

To establish that the **whole function** is close to low-degree, we need some global consistency.

The challenge is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{How can we test global low-degree structure using limited information?}
}
$$

That is the problem that the next stages of our story will solve.

---

# Reed–Solomon Proximity as a Geometric Picture

It is useful to visualize the situation.

The set

$$
\mathbb F^D
$$

contains all possible functions

$$
g:D\to\mathbb F.
$$

Most of these functions are arbitrary. Inside this huge space sits a much smaller set:

$$
RS(D,k).
$$

Those are the functions that come from degree-$<k$ polynomials.

So we can imagine:

$$
\mathbb F^D
\supset
RS(D,k).
$$

A function $g$ can be:

* **inside** the Reed–Solomon code,
* **near** the Reed–Solomon code,
* or **far** from the Reed–Solomon code.

Conceptually:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
\text{all functions}\\[2mm]
\downarrow\\
\text{low-degree functions}\\
\end{array}
}
$$

The problem of Reed–Solomon proximity is simply asking:

> **Where does this function lie relative to the low-degree functions?**

This geometric viewpoint is extremely useful for understanding FRI.

---

# Relative Distance to Reed–Solomon

Let's collect the notation.

Let $D$ be an evaluation domain with $|D|=n$.

Let $RS(D,k)$ be the Reed–Solomon code consisting of evaluations of polynomials of degree less than $k$.

For any function

$$
g:D\to\mathbb F,
$$

define

$$
d_H(g,RS(D,k))
=
\min_{\deg f<k}
|\{x\in D:g(x)\neq f(x)\}|.
$$

The **relative distance**, or normalized distance, is

$$
\boxed{
\Delta(g,RS(D,k))
=
\frac{1}{n}
\min_{\deg f<k}
|\{x\in D:g(x)\neq f(x)\}|.
}
$$

Thus:

$$
\Delta(g,RS(D,k))=0
$$

if and only if

$$
g\in RS(D,k).
$$

And if

$$
\Delta(g,RS(D,k))\le\epsilon,
$$

then there exists a degree-$< k$ polynomial $f$ such that

$$
g(x)=f(x)
$$

on at least

$$
(1-\epsilon)n
$$

points of $D$.

This is the precise mathematical meaning of:

> **$g$ is $\epsilon$-close to a Reed–Solomon codeword.**

# A Crucial Distinction: Polynomial vs. Evaluation Vector

There is another distinction that will become increasingly important.

Suppose

$$
f(X)
=
a_0+a_1X+\cdots+a_{k-1}X^{k-1}
$$

has degree less than $k$.

We can represent it in two ways.

### Coefficient representation

$$
(a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_{k-1}).
$$

### Evaluation representation

$$
\left(
f(x_1),\ldots,f(x_n)
\right).
$$

These are two representations of the same mathematical object.

In FRI, we will usually work with the **evaluation representation**.

Why?

Because the verifier does not necessarily want the prover to send polynomial coefficients. Instead, the prover commits to evaluations of the polynomial over a domain. Then FRI checks whether those evaluations are consistent with a low-degree polynomial.

So our conceptual picture becomes:

$$
\boxed{
\text{polynomial}
\leftrightarrow
\text{evaluation vector}
\leftrightarrow
\text{function on }D.
}
$$

This three-way correspondence is central to everything that follows.

# Summary

The main ideas of this chapter are:

### 1. A function can be viewed as a vector

For

$$
g:D\to\mathbb F,
$$

we can write

$$
g=(g(x_1),\ldots,g(x_n)).
$$

### 2. Reed–Solomon codewords are low-degree functions

$$
RS(D,k)
=
\{f|_D:\deg f<k\}.
$$

### 3. Distance from a function to the code measures how much it must change

$$
d_H(g,RS(D,k))
=
\min_{\deg f<k}
d_H(g,f).
$$

### 4. Relative distance measures the fraction of changed positions

$$
\boxed{
\Delta(g,RS(D,k))
=
\frac{d_H(g,RS(D,k))}{|D|}.
}
$$

### 5. Proximity means agreement with a low-degree polynomial

If

$$
\Delta(g,RS(D,k))\le\epsilon,
$$

then there exists a polynomial $f$ with

$$
\deg f<k
$$

that agrees with $g$ on at least

$$
(1-\epsilon)|D|
$$

points.

### 6. This is different from simply interpolating a few points

Any $k$ points can be interpolated by a degree-$<k$ polynomial.

The real question is whether that polynomial explains **most of the entire domain**.

### 7. This leads to low-degree testing

The problem becomes:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Is }g:D\to\mathbb F
\text{ close to a low-degree polynomial?}
}
$$

And this is the question that eventually motivates FRI.

## The Question We Have Reached

We have now transformed our original coding-theory problem into an algebraic one:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Given }g:D\to\mathbb F,
\text{ is }g\text{ close to a low-degree polynomial?}
}
$$

But there is still a major problem.

The domain $D$ might contain millions of points.

We want a verifier to check low-degree structure **without examining the entire function**.

How can we exploit the algebraic structure of the evaluation domain and the polynomial itself to make this verification efficient?

That is where we go next:

$$
\boxed{
\text{structured evaluation domains}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{polynomial folding}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{FRI}.
}
$$
