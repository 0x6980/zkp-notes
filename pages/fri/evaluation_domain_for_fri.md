# Evaluation Domains for FRI

In the previous chapter, we changed the way we think about Reed–Solomon codes.

Instead of focusing primarily on error correction, we asked:

> Given a function $g:D\to\mathbb F_q$, is it close to the evaluations of a low-degree polynomial?

Mathematically, we were interested in

$$
\Delta(g,RS(D,k)).
$$

This is the question that eventually leads to FRI.

When we say

$$
g\in RS(D,k),
$$

we are making a statement about **both**:

* the function $g$ with degree lest tha $k$,
* and the evaluation domain $D$.

The same values interpreted on a different domain may correspond to a different polynomial behavior.

So in FRI, the prover's object is not merely:

$$
(g(x_1),\ldots,g(x_n)).
$$

It is really:

$$
\boxed{
\text{a function }g\text{ defined on a specific structured domain }D.
}
$$

The domain is part of the algebraic context.

But there is a problem.

Suppose the evaluation domain contains thousands, millions, or even billions of points.

We cannot simply inspect every value of $g$.

We need to exploit some structure.

And this is where the choice of the evaluation domain becomes extremely important.

For a general Reed–Solomon code, the evaluation domain can be any collection of distinct field elements:

$$
D=\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}.
$$

For FRI, however, we choose $D$ very carefully.

The domain is not just a list of points.

In particular, we want the points to naturally come in pairs of the form

$$
\boxed{\{x,-x\}}.
$$

That is, whenever

$$
x\in D,
$$

we also want

$$
-x\in D.
$$

For example, a domain might look like

$$
D=\{x_0,-x_0,x_1,-x_1,x_2,-x_2,\ldots\}.
$$

This gives us a natural pairing:

$$
x_0\leftrightarrow -x_0,
\qquad
x_1\leftrightarrow -x_1,
\qquad
x_2\leftrightarrow -x_2.
$$

There is an additional property we want.

After pairing the points, we consider their squares:

$$
x\mapsto x^2.
$$

Because

$$
x^2=(-x)^2,
$$

each pair \(\{x,-x\}\) maps to a single point.

Therefore, we can define the **squared domain**

$$
\boxed{
D^2=\{x^2:x\in D\}.
}
$$

If the original domain has the right structure, $D^2$ is again a structured domain in which the same kind of pairing can be performed.

So we can repeat the process:

$$
D_0
\longrightarrow
D_1=D_0^2
\longrightarrow
D_2=D_1^2
\longrightarrow
D_3=D_2^2
\longrightarrow\cdots
$$

At each step, the domain becomes smaller.

Schematically, we are looking for a sequence of domains of the form

$$
\boxed{
D_0
\supset
D_1
\supset
D_2
\supset
\cdots
\supset
\{1\},
}
$$

where

$$
\boxed{
D_{i+1}=D_i^2.
}
$$

For example, the sizes might decrease as

$$
16\to8\to4\to2\to1.
$$

The final domain contains only

$$
\boxed{\{1\}}.
$$

So, at a high level, the kind of evaluation domain we are looking for has two important properties:

1. **Its points can be naturally paired as $x$ and $-x$.**
2. **Squaring the points produces another domain with the same kind of structure, allowing the process to be repeated until only \(1\) remains.**

We will not yet explain why FRI needs this particular structure. We will answer that in the next chapter.

The key structure will come from **roots of unity**.

Assume our field $\mathbb F_q$ contains an element

$$
\omega
$$

of order $n$.

That means

$$
\omega^n=1
$$

and

$$
\omega^i\neq1
\qquad
\text{for }0<i<n.
$$

The powers of $\omega$ form the multiplicative subgroup

$$
D=
\langle\omega\rangle
=
\{1,\omega,\omega^2,\ldots,\omega^{n-1}\}.
$$

We will use this subgroup as our evaluation domain.

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
D=
\{1,\omega,\omega^2,\ldots,\omega^{n-1}\}.
}
$$

The domain contains exactly $n$ points.

---

# Why Is This Domain Special?

At first glance, we have simply replaced arbitrary points

$$
x_1,\ldots,x_n
$$

with powers of $\omega$.

But the powers of $\omega$ have an important algebraic relationship.

Every point is generated by the previous point:

$$
\omega^i
\longrightarrow
\omega^{i+1}.
$$

More importantly, if $n$ is even, then

$$
\boxed{
\omega^{n/2}=-1.
}
$$

This means that for every point

$$
x=\omega^i,
$$

the point

$$
-x
$$

is also in the domain:

$$
-x
=
-\omega^i
=
\omega^{n/2}\omega^i
=
\omega^{i+n/2}.
$$

So the domain naturally splits into pairs:

$$
\boxed{
\{x,-x\}.
}
$$

This pairing is exactly what we need for folding.

---

# A Concrete Example

Suppose

$$
n=8.
$$

Let $\omega$ be an element of order \(8\).

Then the domain is

$$
D=
\{
1,
\omega,
\omega^2,
\omega^3,
\omega^4,
\omega^5,
\omega^6,
\omega^7
\}.
$$

Since

$$
\omega^4=-1,
$$

we have the pairs

$$
\{1,-1\},
$$

$$
\{\omega,-\omega\},
$$

$$
\{\omega^2,-\omega^2\},
$$

$$
\{\omega^3,-\omega^3\}.
$$

So eight points can naturally be grouped into four pairs.

This gives us a way to reduce an \(8\)-point domain to a \(4\)-point domain.

That is the first hint of the FRI structure:

$$
8
\longrightarrow
4
\longrightarrow
2
\longrightarrow
1.
$$

At every step, the domain size is roughly halved.

---

# The Smaller Domain

The pairing does more than merely group points.

The squares of the elements of $D$ form another subgroup:

$$
D^2
=
\{x^2:x\in D\}.
$$

If

$$
D=\langle\omega\rangle
$$

has order $n$, then

$$
D^2
=
\langle\omega^2\rangle.
$$

Because $n$ is even, $\omega^2$ has order

$$
\frac n2.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
|D^2|=\frac n2.
}
$$

So squaring maps the original domain

$$
D
$$

onto a smaller domain

$$
D^2.
$$

For example,

$$
D=
\{1,\omega,\omega^2,\ldots,\omega^{7}\}
$$

maps under squaring to

$$
D^2=
\{1,\omega^2,\omega^4,\omega^6\}.
$$

There are four points instead of eight.

Notice something interesting:

$$
x^2=(-x)^2.
$$

Therefore the two points in each pair,

$$
x
\quad\text{and}\quad
-x,
$$

map to the **same point** in the smaller domain.

This is exactly what we want.

The pair

$$
\{x,-x\}
$$

becomes one point

$$
x^2.
$$

So the domain naturally folds in half:

$$
\boxed{
D
\quad\xrightarrow{x\mapsto x^2}\quad
D^2.
}
$$

---

# Cosets: Why We Sometimes Do Not Use the Subgroup Itself

So far, our domain has been

$$
D=\langle\omega\rangle.
$$

But in practice, FRI often works with a **coset** of a multiplicative subgroup.

Choose some nonzero field element

$$
a\in\mathbb F_q^\times.
$$

Then consider

$$
H=aD
=
\{a, a\omega,a\omega^2,\ldots,a\omega^{n-1}\}.
$$

This is a multiplicative coset of $D$.

Why use a coset instead of $D$ itself?

Because cosets preserve essentially the same algebraic structure while allowing us to shift the evaluation domain away from particular special points.

For example, depending on the protocol and field, it may be useful to avoid evaluating at \(0\), or to choose a domain that interacts more conveniently with a polynomial's degree properties.

The important thing for our purposes is:

$$
\boxed{
H=aD
}
$$

still has the same size:

$$
|D|=|H|=n.
$$

And its elements still have a very structured relationship.

---

# Squaring a Coset

Suppose

$$
H=aD.
$$

If $x\in H$, then

$$
-x
$$

is also in $H$, provided the subgroup has even order.

Moreover,

$$
x^2
$$

belongs to

$$
a^2D^2.
$$

Therefore the squaring map takes

$$
aD
$$

to

$$
a^2D^2.
$$

So the squaring process becomes

$$
\boxed{
aD
\quad\xrightarrow{x\mapsto x^2}\quad
a^2D^2.
}
$$

The domain size is halved.

This gives us a sequence of domains:

$$
D_0
\supset
D_1
\supset
D_2
\supset\cdots
$$

where, conceptually,

$$
D_{i+1}=D_i^2.
$$

Each step has roughly half as many points.

This sequence is the **domain structure underlying FRI**.

---

# A Concrete Domain Sequence

Suppose our initial domain has size

$$
n=16.
$$

We can think of the FRI domain sequence as

$$
16
\longrightarrow
8
\longrightarrow
4
\longrightarrow
2
\longrightarrow
1.
$$

More explicitly, if

$$
D_0=\langle\omega\rangle
$$

where $\omega$ has order \(16\), then

$$
D_1=\langle\omega^2\rangle
$$

has order \(8\),

$$
D_2=\langle\omega^4\rangle
$$

has order \(4\),

and so on.

At every step,

$$
|D_{i+1}|=\frac{|D_i|}{2}.
$$

This is why FRI is called a **folding** procedure.

The evaluation domain itself is repeatedly folded in half.

---

# Summary

The main ideas of this chapter are:

### 1. Reed–Solomon codes allow arbitrary distinct evaluation domains

$$
D=\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}.
$$

But FRI needs more structure.

### 2. FRI commonly uses multiplicative subgroups or their cosets

For a subgroup generated by $\omega$,

$$
D=\{1,\omega,\ldots,\omega^{n-1}\}.
$$

### 3. When $n$ is even,

$$
\omega^{n/2}=-1.
$$

Therefore every point $x\in D$ has its partner

$$
-x\in D.
$$

### 4. Squaring identifies each pair

$$
x^2=(-x)^2.
$$

Therefore the domain naturally folds:

$$
D
\longrightarrow
D^2.
$$

The size is reduced from

$$
n
$$

to

$$
\frac n2.
$$
